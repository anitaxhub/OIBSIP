Her story

Augusta Ada Byron was born in London on 10 December 1815, the only legitimate child of the poet Lord Byron and Annabella Milbanke. Her parents’ marriage collapsed soon after she arrived, and she never met her father. Her mother feared Ada might inherit his restless temperament, so she filled her daughter’s lessons with mathematics, music and languages. It was a choice that quietly shaped a future.

Mathematics turned out to be far more than a school subject for Ada. Through her tutor, the scientist Mary Somerville, she met the inventor Charles Babbage in 1833 and was captivated by a working piece of his Difference Engine. The two became lifelong friends and wrote to each other for years about machines that did not yet exist, while Ada kept studying advanced mathematics with mentors such as Augustus De Morgan.

Her best-known work came in 1842 and 1843. Babbage had designed the Analytical Engine, a much more ambitious machine meant to follow instructions fed in on punched cards. Ada translated an article about it by the Italian engineer Luigi Menabrea, then added seven notes of her own that ended up longer than the article itself. In the last of them, Note G, she set out step by step how the engine could calculate Bernoulli numbers, a sequence widely regarded as the first published computer program.

What makes her writing remarkable is its imagination. Ada understood that a machine working with symbols, and not only numbers, could in principle handle music or any other kind of information. She called her way of thinking “poetical science”. She died on 27 November 1852, aged just 36, and the engine was never finished. Yet her notes were rediscovered in the 1950s, and today a programming language and an annual day in October carry her name.

Key moments

  1. 1815

    Born in London on 10 December, the daughter of Lord Byron and Annabella Milbanke.

  2. 1833

    Meets Charles Babbage through Mary Somerville and sees a prototype of his Difference Engine.

  3. 1835

    Marries William King. When he is made Earl of Lovelace in 1838, Ada becomes the Countess of Lovelace.

  4. 1843

    Publishes her translation of Menabrea’s article with seven Notes (A to G), signed with the initials AAL. Note G contains her Bernoulli number method.

  5. 1852

    Dies in London on 27 November at the age of 36.

  6. 1950s

    Her Notes on the Analytical Engine are republished and her ideas are rediscovered by a new generation.

  7. Today

    The Ada programming language is named after her, and Ada Lovelace Day on the second Tuesday of October celebrates women in science, technology, engineering and maths.

The Analytical Engine weaves algebraic patterns, just as the Jacquard loom weaves flowers and leaves.
